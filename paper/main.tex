\begin{abstract}
\noindent Most AI products in 2026 rent intelligence through APIs. This essay asks whether
organizations will increasingly own and adapt their models instead, and answers from public
prices and surveys, a cost model and three experiments. Ownership is growing, but as an owned
layer of narrow, adapted models beneath a rented frontier rather than as a replacement for it.
For one narrow task moved from a frontier API to a fine-tuned open 8B model, a dynamic
break-even simulation finds owning cheaper in half of its draws at a monthly API bill of about
\$22,000, or about \$37,000 at US labor rates. That even-odds point is a property of the
simulation's assumed input ranges, not the odds facing any particular organization. Ignoring the
falling price of rented capability puts it about seven times too low, and lifetime
savings from a price advantage are capped at roughly half a year of the bill replaced. Labor,
not compute, sets the threshold. Three experiments on training data show that defects cost
money mainly through silent harm. Deterministic validators catch structural defects but not
semantic ones; a widely used training library trains silently on most structural defects at
its default settings; and on a small model, systematic label errors cost up to 32 points of
accuracy. Code, data hashes and seeds are released at
\url{https://github.com/Thatayotlhe04/a-parallel-paradigm}.
\end{abstract}
